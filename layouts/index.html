Handing a Supplier Conversation to a Colleague Without Losing the Open Questions

A purchasing conversation needs a usable handover when its usual contact is away or changes roles. Give the next person the current brief, the latest supplier response, a short list of unresolved questions and a clear limit on what they may approve. A forwarded inbox is useful background, but it does not tell a colleague which statements still apply. This guide describes a practical handover for a supplier inquiry, before any new commitment is made.

1. Identify the conversation being transferred

Start with the subject of the inquiry, the buyer's internal reference and the supplier contact already involved. Include the date of the last meaningful exchange. These details help the receiving colleague distinguish this conversation from a similar inquiry with another configuration or destination.

Refer to the existing conversation when contacting the supplier again; starting an unrelated thread can leave the supplier unsure whether the new message replaces the old request or introduces another one.

A useful handover begins with a short description: “We asked for a quotation against brief B, dated 4 November. We have received questions about the contents of the kit and have not yet answered them.” This is a fictional example, not a statement about a TBPHP order. It tells the next contact both where the discussion stands and why work remains.

Do not describe the conversation as an approved order just because several messages discuss quantities. An inquiry, a quotation under review and a confirmed order are different stages. Use the stage supported by the records you actually have.

2. Separate the current documents from the background

Create a small current-document list. For each item, record its filename, date, purpose and status. A purchasing brief might be current, a proposed label might still need review, and a previous quotation might be retained only to explain a change.

Keep the previous material accessible where your company's access rules permit. Moving a file into a folder named “old” is not enough if the handover still links to it as the source of the current quantity. Follow each link and check what it opens before passing the conversation on.

Use a sentence to explain each replacement. “Brief B replaces brief A for kit contents; the destination has not changed” is more useful than “updated file attached.” The next person can see the scope of the revision without comparing every paragraph immediately.

If two attachments disagree and no recorded decision resolves the difference, mark the issue as unresolved. Do not choose the newer-looking file merely because its filename sorts last. Ask the original owner or the relevant approver to identify the controlling version before the colleague sends an answer based on it.

3. Give every open question a next action

An open-question list should say what information is missing, who is expected to provide it and what the receiving colleague should do next. “Packaging pending” is too broad. It might refer to artwork, quantities, dimensions or the identity of the person who can approve a proposed change.

The following fictional handover uses plain language rather than a complicated status system:

  • Kit contents: the supplier asked whether the accessory is included in every kit. Buyer product owner must answer; the new contact should obtain that decision before replying.
  • Label file: two versions are attached. Buyer artwork owner must identify the current one; neither version is approved in this example.
  • Quotation scope: the earlier request did not distinguish samples from a later order. The new contact should clarify the scope in the next message.

Keep the supplier's actual question alongside your summary if the wording matters. A short paraphrase can accidentally remove a condition, such as “if the accessory is included” or “subject to the revised artwork.” Those conditions often explain why an answer is still pending.

Avoid assigning an invented response deadline to the supplier. Record an agreed date if one exists. Otherwise, label the date as the buyer's desired review date and ask whether it is workable.

4. State what the new contact can approve

The person answering emails may not be the person authorized to approve a specification change or a purchase. Make that distinction explicit in the internal handover. List the decisions the colleague can make and the decisions that require another person's review, using your organization's actual approval rules.

For example, the new contact might collect missing information and arrange a review, while the product owner approves the configuration. This is an illustrative division of work, not a recommendation to replace an existing approval process.

The supplier-facing introduction should be shorter than the internal handover. Identify the new contact, the inquiry being continued and any relevant approval boundary. There is usually no need to forward internal discussion about staffing, budgets or unrelated customers to establish who will answer the next question.

Have the colleague read the latest supplier message and explain the next action back in their own words. If their understanding differs from the handover, resolve that difference before they reply. This simple check catches missing context while the original owner is still available.

5. Carry private-label decisions with their status

A private-label discussion can contain several separate decisions: what the buyer requested, what the supplier has reviewed and what someone has actually approved. Preserve those distinctions when transferring the conversation. A label attached for discussion should not become “final artwork” in the handover unless approval is recorded.

The handover still needs the specific request and correspondence for the inquiry being transferred. A general service description does not establish acceptance of a particular design, configuration or delivery date.

For each unresolved customization item, include the relevant file and the last question asked about it. State whether the next step belongs to the buyer or supplier. If no one has reviewed an item yet, say so. This makes the unfinished work visible without suggesting a decision has already been made.

When the colleague sends the next message, keep that message focused on the current questions. Repeating every discarded idea can restart discussions that were deliberately closed.

6. Close the handover with a dated acknowledgment

Ask the receiving colleague to acknowledge the handover internally and name the first action they will take. Record the date and keep the note with the inquiry. This is an administrative check, not proof that the supplier has accepted any new terms.

If the original contact remains copied for a short transition, explain who is now responsible for responding. Otherwise, both people may assume the other person will answer. When the handover is complete, keep a clear owner rather than two informal owners with overlapping responsibilities.

Before sending anything outward, check the recipient, attachments and current brief once more. A well-written summary cannot prevent confusion if the wrong attachment travels with it.

Frequently asked questions

Is forwarding the whole email thread enough?

It supplies background, but it may leave the next contact to infer the current status. Add a short summary of the active brief, unanswered questions and approval limits. Keep the full thread available as supporting context, particularly where an earlier message explains a condition or change.

What if the original contact is unavailable?

Build the handover from the records that can be verified, and mark gaps explicitly. The receiving colleague should confirm unresolved points with the appropriate buyer contact or supplier before acting on them. Absence of an answer should remain an open issue rather than becoming an assumed approval.

Should the supplier receive the internal handover document?

Usually a focused introduction and the relevant current documents are enough. Review any proposed attachment for internal comments, unrelated conversations and information the recipient does not need. The purpose is to establish continuity and the next action, while preserving the buyer's normal controls over information sharing.
